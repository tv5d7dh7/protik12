01 Why these three

Some trips need a map. Some need a door.

A travel guide is not a list of places. It is a way of seeing — a set of instructions for leaving your own century, your own country, your own habits at the border.

These three do different jobs. One takes you back to the road as it was in the Middle Ages: pilgrims, spies, merchants and saints, with advice on currency, seasickness and singing sirens. One takes you through Italy in 2026 with maps, itineraries and opinions sharp enough to tell you what to skip. One takes you to Japan through a decade of living there — not a visit, a life.

They do not overlap. They do not need to be read in order. Pick the door you need.

03 Field notes

Three places to start before you go.

Not an itinerary. A first sentence — the kind that makes you book the flight, or finally open the book.

Field note 01 41.9°N · 12.5°E

Rome, 1350

The medieval traveller’s Rome was more ruin than tourist site. Bale’s guide is the closest thing to a visa for the fourteenth century: take the exchange rate, leave the guidebook, expect the sirens.

Read with Volume I
Field note 02 40.3°N · 18.1°E

Puglia, 2026

Frommer’s sends you south when everyone else is queueing in Florence. Start in Lecce, end with a long breakfast at a masseria, and use the book’s “what to skip” notes without guilt.

Read with Volume II
Field note 03 38.3°N · 140.9°E

Tohoku, 2012

Broad’s Japan begins in a rural village, not Shibuya. The best chapter is the one about staying: the slow accumulation of a place that stops being foreign and starts being home.

Read with Volume III
